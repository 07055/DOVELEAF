Here is the exact, universal framework we use to remove blood stains from clothing, bedding, and fabrics of every kind — from fresh stains that happened five minutes ago to dried stains that have been sitting for weeks. Blood stains consistently cause the most panic, and in nearly every case the customer's first instinct — reaching for hot water and bleach — will make the problem dramatically worse.

## The Science: Why Hot Water Destroys Blood Removal

Blood is a **protein-based stain**. The red colour comes from haemoglobin, an iron-containing protein. When haemoglobin meets heat, it denatures — the protein unfolds and bonds aggressively with fabric fibres. This is the same process that happens when you cook an egg: the clear albumin turns white and solid because heat permanently changes its molecular structure.

Once blood proteins are heat-denatured in fabric, they become virtually impossible to remove without professional enzymatic treatment. This is why the first and most critical rule of blood stain removal is:

**Never use hot water. Never. Not even warm. Cold only.**

## Fresh Blood Stains (Within One Hour)

Fresh blood is by far the easiest to remove because the proteins have not yet fully bonded with the fabric fibres. Speed matters enormously.

### Step 1: Rinse Immediately With Cold Water

Hold the stained fabric under **cold running water** — as cold as your tap can produce. Direct the water from the **back of the stain**, pushing the blood out of the fabric rather than through it. The goal is to flush as much liquid blood out as possible before it dries.

Do not rub the fabric against itself. Do not use soap yet. Just cold water and gentle pressure.

### Step 2: Apply Hydrogen Peroxide (For White and Light Fabrics)

**Hydrogen peroxide (3% pharmacy grade)** is the single most effective household product for fresh blood stains. It works through oxidation — the peroxide molecule breaks the chemical bonds in haemoglobin, dissolving the protein and lifting the colour simultaneously.

1. Pour hydrogen peroxide directly onto the stain.
2. You will see it fizz and bubble — this is the peroxide reacting with the blood proteins. This is normal and desirable.
3. Blot with a clean cloth as the fizzing subsides. The cloth will pick up the dissolved stain.
4. Repeat until the fizzing stops and the stain is gone or nearly gone.
5. Rinse with cold water.

**Important:** Hydrogen peroxide can lighten some dyed fabrics. Test on an inconspicuous area first. For dark or brightly coloured garments, skip this step and use the enzymatic method below instead.

### Step 3: For Coloured Fabrics — Use Cold Water and Dish Soap

1. Apply a small drop of mild liquid dish soap directly to the stain.
2. Gently work it in with your fingertips using cold water.
3. Let it sit for fifteen minutes.
4. Rinse thoroughly with cold water.

Dish soap is surfactant-based and will emulsify the blood proteins without the oxidative risk that hydrogen peroxide poses to dyes.

## Dried Blood Stains (Older Than 24 Hours)

Dried blood is significantly harder to remove because the proteins have oxidised and bonded with the fabric. The haemoglobin has changed from its soluble liquid state to an insoluble solid state. You need to re-dissolve the protein before you can remove it.

### The Soak Method

1. **Submerge the stained item in cold water.** Use a basin or bucket, not a running tap — the fabric needs sustained contact with water to rehydrate the dried blood.

2. **Add enzyme-based laundry detergent** — the kind marketed as "stain remover" or "bio detergent" contains protease enzymes that specifically break down protein-based stains. Add approximately two tablespoons per litre of water.

3. **Soak for two to six hours.** For severely dried stains, extend to overnight. The enzymes need time to digest the hardened protein.

4. **After soaking**, gently agitate the fabric in the water. You should see the water turning pinkish as the dissolved blood releases from the fibres.

5. **Drain and inspect.** If the stain persists, apply hydrogen peroxide (if the fabric is white or light) or dish soap (if coloured) and repeat.

6. **Wash as normal** in cold water with your regular detergent.

## For Delicate Fabrics: Silk, Wool, and Cashmere

These fabrics require special handling because they are themselves protein-based — the same enzymes that break down blood proteins can also damage silk and wool fibres.

### Silk

1. Lay the silk flat and place a white cloth underneath the stain.
2. Dab the stain with a cloth dampened in cold water mixed with a few drops of mild dish soap.
3. Rinse by dabbing with a separate cold-water-damp cloth.
4. Never submerge silk for extended periods — it weakens when saturated.
5. Air dry flat, away from sunlight.

### Wool and Cashmere

1. Submerge in cold water with a wool-specific detergent (not enzyme-based — enzymes will attack wool fibres).
2. Soak for no more than thirty minutes.
3. Gently press — never rub or wring.
4. Roll in a towel to absorb moisture and lay flat to dry.

## Blood on Bedding and Mattresses

Blood stains on sheets and pillowcases are common and straightforward — these items are almost always white cotton, so you can use the full hydrogen peroxide method.

For **mattress stains**, you cannot submerge the item. Instead:

1. Mix a paste of **cold water, hydrogen peroxide, and baking soda**.
2. Apply to the stain and let it sit for thirty minutes.
3. Blot with a cold damp cloth until the paste and dissolved blood are removed.
4. Allow to air dry completely before remaking the bed.

## Blood on Denim

Raw and dark-wash denim presents a unique challenge: you need to remove the blood without stripping the indigo dye.

1. Use the cold-water soak with enzyme detergent, but keep the soak to **one hour maximum** — extended soaking can begin dissolving indigo.
2. Rinse with cold water only.
3. Avoid hydrogen peroxide — it can lighten the denim and create a visible spot.
4. If the stain persists after one treatment, repeat rather than extending the soak time.

## The Spoon-Scrape Trick for Dried Blood

For stubborn dried blood stains that resist the soak method, try this technique used by professional cleaners:

1. Wet the stained area with cold water.
2. Stretch the fabric taut over a bowl or your hand.
3. Gently scrape the surface of the stain with a **dull knife or spoon edge**. This mechanically removes the top layer of dried blood protein that the enzymes have not fully digested.
4. Re-soak and repeat.

This works because dried blood sits primarily on the surface of the fibre — physical removal of the top layer allows the enzymes to reach the remaining bonded protein underneath.

## Prevention and Quick Response

Keep a small **hydrogen peroxide spray bottle** in your first aid kit and your laundry area. When a blood stain occurs, an immediate spray of hydrogen peroxide followed by a cold rinse will handle most incidents before they become stains at all.

For families with children, this single habit will save more clothing than any other garment care technique.

## When to See a Professional

- **Blood on wedding dresses or formal wear** — the risk of fabric damage is too high for home treatment
- **Blood on antique textiles** — vintage fabrics may be fragile and require gentle solvent treatment
- **Stains that have been through a dryer** — heat-set blood proteins need industrial enzymatic cleaners
- **Large-area blood contamination** — mattresses, upholstery, or carpet that require professional extraction equipment
