Here is the exact, universal framework we use to iron clothes properly — the temperature settings for every fabric type, the pressing cloth technique that prevents shine marks, and the sequence for ironing a dress shirt in under five minutes.

Ironing is not about heat alone — it is about the combination of **heat, steam, pressure, and time.** Understanding how these four variables interact for each fabric type is the difference between a professional result and a damaged garment.

## The Temperature Settings: Your Complete Guide

Every iron has a temperature dial with symbols or numbers. Here is exactly what each setting means and which fabrics it serves:

### Low Heat (110 degrees Celsius / 230 degrees Fahrenheit)

**The symbol:** One dot

**Fabrics:** Silk, nylon, acrylic, acetate

These fabrics are highly heat-sensitive. Silk scorches at relatively low temperatures, and synthetic fibres like nylon and acrylic will melt or develop permanent glaze marks if exposed to excessive heat.

**Key rule:** Never let the iron rest on these fabrics. Keep it moving in smooth, continuous strokes.

### Medium Heat (150 degrees Celsius / 300 degrees Fahrenheit)

**The symbol:** Two dots

**Fabrics:** Wool, polyester blends, rayon, viscose

Wool requires medium heat with steam — the steam relaxes the fibres without the risk of scorching. Polyester blends can tolerate medium heat briefly, but prolonged contact will create a shiny, flattened surface.

**Key rule:** Always iron wool on the reverse side or through a pressing cloth to prevent shine marks.

### High Heat (200 degrees Celsius / 390 degrees Fahrenheit)

**The symbol:** Three dots

**Fabrics:** Cotton, linen

Cotton and linen are the most heat-tolerant natural fibres. They require high heat and generous steam to relax their stubborn wrinkles. Low or medium heat will barely affect a wrinkle in cotton — you need the full temperature.

**Key rule:** Cotton and linen should be slightly damp when ironed. Dry cotton resists ironing; damp cotton yields easily.

## The Pressing Cloth: Your Insurance Policy

A **pressing cloth** is a thin piece of cotton fabric (a handkerchief, a piece of muslin, or a clean tea towel) placed between the iron and the garment. It prevents:

- **Shine marks** on wool and dark fabrics
- **Scorch marks** on delicate fabrics
- **Glaze marks** on synthetic fabrics
- **Direct contact** between the iron's soleplate and embellishments, buttons, or zippers

**When to use a pressing cloth:**

- Always on wool and dark fabrics
- Always on silk and delicate fabrics
- When ironing over pockets, seams, or buttons (to prevent raised impressions on the front)
- When ironing any fabric you are unsure about

**How to use it:**

1. Place the damp pressing cloth over the garment section you are about to iron.
2. Iron over the pressing cloth with normal pressure and steam.
3. Lift the pressing cloth and check the result.
4. Reposition and repeat.

This adds ten to fifteen seconds per section but prevents irreversible damage.

## The Ironing Sequence: Dress Shirt in Under Five Minutes

Professional ironers follow a specific sequence that minimises re-wrinkling of already-ironed sections. This is the sequence used by pressing professionals worldwide:

### Step 1: Collar (30 seconds)

1. Unbutton the collar and lay it flat.
2. Iron from the **points inward** toward the centre — this prevents the collar tips from creasing.
3. Flip and iron the reverse side.

### Step 2: Yoke and Shoulders (30 seconds)

1. Drape the shoulder area over the narrow end of the ironing board.
2. Iron the yoke (the panel across the shoulders) from one side to the other.
3. Repeat on the other shoulder.

### Step 3: Cuffs (30 seconds)

1. Unbutton the cuffs and open them flat.
2. Iron from the seam outward to prevent creases.
3. Iron the sleeve placket (the opening above the cuff).

### Step 4: Sleeves (1 minute)

1. Lay the sleeve flat with the **seam aligned along the bottom edge.**
2. Iron from the shoulder to the cuff in smooth strokes.
3. **Do not create a crease** along the sleeve edge unless the garment's style specifically calls for it (formal shirts only).
4. Repeat on the other sleeve.

### Step 5: Front Panels (1 minute)

1. Button the shirt up — this keeps the front aligned and prevents the button placket from shifting.
2. Iron the **buttonhole side first** — lay it flat and iron around the buttonholes.
3. Flip to the **button side** — iron around the buttons, not over them. Ironing over buttons creates impressions on the back of the fabric and can damage the buttons.

### Step 6: Back Panel (1 minute)

1. Lay the back flat, stretching it taut across the board.
2. Iron from the **yoke down to the hem** in smooth, overlapping strokes.
3. Pay attention to the side seams — iron them flat to create clean lines.

### Step 7: Final Touch (30 seconds)

1. Hang the shirt immediately on a hanger.
2. Button the top button to maintain the collar shape.
3. Allow any residual steam to evaporate before placing in the wardrobe.

## Ironing Trousers

Trousers require a different sequence because of the **crease line** (the sharp fold running down the front of each leg).

### Step 1: Turn Inside Out

Iron the inside of the trousers first to address pockets, waistband, and lining without risking surface damage.

### Step 2: Pockets

Smooth the pocket bags flat and iron them. Crumpled pockets create visible lumps on the outside of the trousers.

### Step 3: Waistband and Fly

Iron the waistband by rotating it around the narrow end of the board. Iron the fly area carefully, avoiding zippers and buttons.

### Step 4: Legs

1. Turn the trousers right-side out.
2. **Align the inner and outer seams** so the crease line runs straight down the front centre of each leg.
3. Iron each leg from the **hem to the waist** — ironing upward prevents the hem from creating a crease at the bottom.
4. Use steam generously on the crease line to set it sharply.

### Step 5: Hang Immediately

Hang trousers on a clip hanger by the cuff or waistband. The weight of the fabric maintains the crease while the garment cools.

## Ironing Specific Fabrics

### Silk

- Lowest heat setting
- Iron while slightly damp or through a damp pressing cloth
- Iron on the reverse side to protect the lustre
- Never spray water directly on silk — it creates spots

### Wool

- Medium heat with steam
- Always use a pressing cloth
- Iron on the reverse side
- Never iron dry wool — the fibres will flatten and shine
- Allow ironed wool to cool before moving — wrinkles set as the fibres cool

### Linen

- Highest heat setting with generous steam
- Iron while damp for best results
- Iron on the reverse side for dark linen
- Linen wrinkles quickly after ironing — iron immediately before wearing

### Polyester and Synthetics

- Low to medium heat
- Use a pressing cloth to prevent glazing
- Quick passes — do not let the iron rest
- Steam is generally more effective than pressure for synthetics

### Denim

- High heat with steam
- Iron on the reverse side to prevent shine on dark denim
- For raw denim, iron inside out to preserve the indigo surface
- Iron the crease lines if desired for a crisp look

## Common Ironing Mistakes

### Ironing Over Buttons

This creates permanent impressions on the fabric behind the buttons and can crack or melt plastic buttons. Always iron around buttons, using the tip of the iron to navigate between them.

### Using Too Much Water on Dark Fabrics

Water marks on dark fabrics are visible and difficult to remove. Use steam rather than water spray on dark garments, and always test on a hidden area first.

### Not Cleaning the Iron's Soleplate

Built-up starch, fabric residue, and mineral deposits on the iron's soleplate can transfer to garments, creating brown marks and snags. Clean the soleplate monthly by ironing over a damp cloth sprinkled with baking soda, or use a commercial iron cleaner.

### Ironing in the Wrong Order

If you iron the back of a shirt first and then the front, the back will re-wrinkle while you work on the front. Follow the professional sequence — collar, yoke, cuffs, sleeves, front, back — to minimise re-wrinkling.

## Ironing in Nairobi's Climate

Nairobi's moderate humidity means garments generally hold their ironed shape well once cooled. However:

- **Iron just before wearing** for the crispest results — Nairobi's dust can dull freshly ironed garments if they sit in the wardrobe for days
- **Store ironed shirts on hangers** rather than folded to maintain the press
- **Use steam generously** — Nairobi's altitude means water boils at a slightly lower temperature, so steam is your primary wrinkle-removal tool
